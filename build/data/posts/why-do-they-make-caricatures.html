People sometimes look at my drawings and ask, half amused and half suspicious, why I bother. Why spend an evening drawing a man in sunglasses with his feet on the desk, selling architect's stamps "slightly used", when I could be drawing a nice façade or a tree? The honest answer is that the façade doesn't need me. The man with the stamps does.

Caricature is an old trade, and it has always had a price. In 1831 Honoré Daumier drew King Louis-Philippe as Gargantua, a giant swallowing the taxes of the poor, and spent six months in prison for it. Around the same time his editor, Charles Philipon, stood in court and showed in four quick sketches how the king's face slid, line by line, into a pear. The judges were not amused. Paris was, and pears started appearing on walls all over the city. I love that story because it says what a good caricature is: a small drawing that the powerful cannot answer without looking ridiculous.

A cheerful art that feeds on sad things

Here is the paradox. Very little of what I draw is happy. Clients who don't pay half the fee upfront. People who still call on a Saturday, knowing perfectly well I don't work weekends. Riot police charging into a crowd under a banner. And yet I never draw any of it in a bad mood. Something happens between the irritation and the paper. The pen softens before it bites.

Exaggeration is a strange kind of generosity. You enlarge the belly, the cigar, the ego, and the reader starts laughing before realising he has just agreed with you. A long argument asks for patience and invites a counter-argument. A drawing skips both steps. It lands, people smile, and the point is already inside.

Black humour goes one floor lower. Laughing at something grim doesn't make it lighter; it makes it visible. When I drew international observers peering through binoculars at a police charge and muttering "it's not really a dictatorship… but it's pretty shocking", I wasn't trying to cheer anyone up. I wanted the embarrassment of that sentence to be heard. A joke that hurts a little is the one people remember on the way home.

Criticism without the lecture

Not every caricature is a political act. Many of mine are just a complaint with good lines. Files named final.dwg, final2.dwg, new final.dwg, really final.dwg. Emails sent with an attachment and no subject. Software some people still teach as if it were 2006. I could write a long post on each of these. Nobody would read it, and I wouldn't blame them. Drawn, it takes two seconds, and every architect who has lived it recognises himself before reaching the caption.

That economy is the real strength of the genre. One image, no footnotes, no "on the other hand". It forces the person drawing to decide what actually matters, which is a good discipline for anyone who criticises anything.

Still, a raised eyebrow is a form of judgment. The impostor at his desk, with his old computer, his stamps for sale and his clients who "trust him", is a type every architect in the region has met. I name no one, and the drawing doesn't need names. Caricature at its best goes after a behaviour, not a person's face or origins. When it starts mocking what people are rather than what they do, it stops being caricature and becomes something cheaper.

The rules I try to keep

Exaggerate what is true; never invent. The moment a caricature lies, it loses the only weapon it has, which is recognition. Laugh at yourself as often as at others, and a good part of my gallery is me: tired, counting the days to the end of a project, relieved when Friday comes. And accept that some people won't laugh. A drawing that pleases everyone has usually missed its target.

So why do they make caricatures? For the same reason people complain to friends over coffee, only with fewer words and better lines. And because a pear, drawn well enough, once frightened a king.
