“You left Paris for this?” On coming home

November 28, 2026 · Karim Ben Khalifa

"You left Paris? For this?" I have heard that question in more than one version. Sometimes it's a raised eyebrow at a wedding, sometimes a cousin's polite silence, sometimes a straight "why would anyone do that?". I worked as an architect in Paris until 2023. Then I came back to Menzel Abderrahmen, my family's village near Bizerte, and opened my own office. For a lot of people, that only makes sense as a failure.

The script is familiar. You go north, you stay, you send photos from the metro, and the family talks about the child who is "over there" the way people talk about a diploma on the wall. Going the other way breaks the script. Nobody has to say it's a step down. The questions do the job.

The paradise people describe

Europe has real things to offer, and I won't pretend otherwise. Trains that leave on time, offices that answer letters, salaries that go further. Paris gave me a lot. But a place can work very well and still leave you feeling like a guest who has stayed a bit too long. You learn to explain your name, your food, your fasting, your prayer break. You learn which jokes to let pass. None of it is dramatic. It just adds up, day after day, and it rarely shows up when people compare countries.

Frantz Fanon, the Martinican psychiatrist who worked in Algeria, spent much of his writing on how colonial hierarchies settle inside people's heads. In Black Skin, White Masks (1952) he describes the man who comes home from France changed, speaking differently, looking down on those who stayed. Decades after independence, the reflex is still around. It has only flipped: now the one who comes back is the odd one, and the one who stays abroad is the success story. Both versions measure a life by how close it sits to the old metropole.

What the novels already knew

Tayeb Salih's Season of Migration to the North (1966) is the book I would hand to anyone who thinks the road north is simple. Its narrator comes back to his village on the Nile after seven years of study in England and meets Mustafa Sa'eed, a man who conquered London's lecture halls and salons and came out of it broken, hiding in a Sudanese village. The prestige was real. So was the wreckage. Salih himself lived most of his life in London, which is part of why the book rings true: he wasn't preaching return, he was showing what the trip can cost.

Much older, Homer told the same story from the other side. Odysseus spends seven years on Calypso's island. She offers him immortality and eternal youth if he stays. He sits on the shore every day, staring at the sea, and in the end he builds a raft and leaves. Later he says it plainly (Samuel Butler's translation):

"There is nothing dearer to a man than his own country and his parents, and however splendid a home he may have in a foreign country, if it be far from father or mother, he does not care about it." — Homer, The Odyssey, Book IX

Coming home is not running away from the real world. Sometimes it's simply refusing to live a life borrowed from someone else's idea of success.

What counts, from where I stand

As a Muslim, I was taught that a person's worth isn't measured by a passport or a postcode. It's measured by taqwa, awareness of Allah, and you can lose it or keep it anywhere. I know Muslims who live their faith beautifully in Paris, and people here who have let theirs slip. Geography guarantees nothing.

Still, it helps to live where the adhan is part of the everyday noise, where Ramadan is the whole town's rhythm and not a personal exception you explain to your manager. Small things. They make faith lighter to carry, and for me that weighs more than a bigger salary. Some would call it barakah, or sakinah. I call it feeling at home.

The Quran is clear about where dignity comes from:

"And We have certainly honored the children of Adam and carried them on the land and sea and provided for them of the good things..." — Quran 17:70 (Sahih International)

Honour is given. You don't earn it by moving to the right city. And the Prophet ﷺ described how a believer meets whatever comes his way:

"How wonderful is the affair of the believer, for his affairs are all good, and this applies to no one but the believer. If something good happens to him, he is grateful, and that is good for him. If something bad happens to him, he bears it with patience, and that is good for him." — Sahih Muslim, 2999

Read that way, the question "did you succeed or fail?" loses most of its weight. Paris was good for me. Coming back is good for me. I learned in one place and I build in the other.

Gratitude over applause

I don't need everyone to agree. Those who see Europe as a paradise will keep measuring life with their ruler, and I'll keep measuring mine with alhamdulillah. They're not the same tool, and arguing about it over coffee changes nothing.
